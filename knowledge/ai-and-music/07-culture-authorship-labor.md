# Culture, authorship and labour

**TL;DR for the assistant:** The copyright fight is the surface; the real argument is whether music means anything without a person meaning it. Nick Cave calls AI songwriting "a grotesque mockery of what it is to be human" and says ChatGPT "is fast-tracking the commodification of the human spirit by mechanising the imagination" — while Holly Herndon treats machine learning as "a really interesting and wonderful new human coordination technique" and credits her AI, Spawn, as an ensemble member. History rhymes: the American Federation of Musicians lost about 22,000 theatre jobs to talking pictures after 1927 and founded a Music Defense League in 1930 to fight "canned music"; the Linn LM-1 in 1980 was feared to put every session drummer in Los Angeles out of work; Auto-Tune was voted one of Time's 50 worst inventions in 2010 and is now just an instrument. The measurable damage today is concentrated, not universal — CISAC projects 24% of music creators' revenue at risk by 2028 and about 60% of *music library* revenue, and 66% of UK music creators say AI threatens their career. And there is a genuine equity problem inside the models themselves: only 5.7% of the hours in existing music-generation datasets come from non-Western genres.

## Key facts

- **Nick Cave, January 2023 (Red Hand Files #218):** responding to a ChatGPT-written "Nick Cave song," he wrote that "ChatGPT may be able to write a speech or an essay or a sermon or an obituary but it cannot create a genuine song," because "songs arise out of suffering... predicated upon the complex, internal human struggle of creation." ([The Red Hand Files #218](https://www.theredhandfiles.com/chat-gpt-what-do-you-think/))
- **Cave's second, sharper letter, August 2023 (#248)** is the *process vs artifact* argument in its purest form: "ChatGPT's intent is to eliminate the process of creation and its attendant challenges, viewing it as nothing more than a time-wasting inconvenience that stands in the way of the commodity itself," and "ChatGPT is fast-tracking the commodification of the human spirit by mechanising the imagination." ([The Red Hand Files #248](https://www.theredhandfiles.com/chatgpt-making-things-faster-and-easier/))
- **Suno CEO Mikey Shulman made the opposite case out loud, 16 January 2025**, on the 20VC podcast: "I think the majority of people don't enjoy the majority of the time they spend making music." That single sentence is the cleanest statement of the "artifact matters, process is friction" position anyone in the industry has given. He later said he wished he had chosen different words. ([RouteNote, 16 Jan 2025](https://routenote.com/blog/sunos-ceo-claims-making-music-is-not-enjoyable/))
- **Brian Eno, January 2025:** he tested a generator trained on his own work and concluded "none of it was so good that I thought, 'Oh my God, I've got to release this.'" His test for AI art: "Somebody said to me the other day, 'I'll be interested in AI when some product of AI makes me cry', and I thought that was a very good test... I think it's not possible unless you assume intentionality on the part of something being made." He also warns you must "stop it going down into the chasm of mediocrity that it will always want to go into, because that's the way it's set up." ([Ultimate Classic Rock, 13 Jan 2025, reporting The Guardian](https://ultimateclassicrock.com/brian-eno-ai-music/))
- **Holly Herndon is the strongest pro-machine artist voice** and she is not a naïf: "I see machine learning as a really interesting and wonderful new human coordination technique... along a continuum of something like early vocal traditions." On Spawn, the model she trained on her own voice: "We really see her as part of our ensemble, an ensemble member, a performer." Her warning is about attribution, not machines: "If we are just hoovering up our shared past archive and then we have a corporate recreation of subcultures that we can consume and never pay attribution to... then we have a really dystopian vision of the future of music." ([Goethe-Institut interview](https://www.goethe.de/prj/k40/en/mus/hol.html))
- **Ted Gioia named the aesthetic, 25 February 2025:** "Slop art is flat, awkward, stale, listless, and often ridiculous." His core claim is ontological: "AI does not possess a self. It lacks personhood... So any art it creates will inevitably feel empty and hollow." And on abundance: "The supply is endless—because AI never sleeps." ([The Honest Broker, 25 Feb 2025](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/the-new-aesthetics-of-slop))
- **Gioia has been making the same argument since 1988** — he republished it on 30 January 2026 — and adds that the word "slop" is itself evidence: "People call this stuff *slop* because they know instinctively how different it is from human creative work. They feel repulsion when confronted by it." ([The Honest Broker, 30 Jan 2026](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/my-warning-about-ai-music-from-1988))
- **Damon Krukowski (Galaxie 500) reframes the whole thing as labour, not copyright.** In October 2023 he argued musicians cannot do what the WGA and SAG-AFTRA did, because independent musicians are classified as independent contractors and coordinated action risks antitrust liability: they "can't even talk about coordinated collective action against our corporate overlords." In September 2025 he added that the majors "have sued and are now negotiating with AI-music generating platforms Suno and Udio... in order to similarly take a piece of the AI action, rather than protecting art." ([Solidarity, 3 Oct 2023](https://dadadrummer.substack.com/p/solidarity); [Artificial Labor, 2 Sep 2025](https://dadadrummer.substack.com/p/artificial-labor))
- **Krukowski's diagnosis got vindicated on 5 June 2026**, when the American Federation of Musicians sued Universal and Warner in the Southern District of New York — not the AI companies, the *labels* — alleging their Suno/Udio settlements licensed members' recordings without compensating or crediting the session players, triggering the "new use" provision (Article 21) of the union's collective bargaining agreement. An amended complaint on 24 July 2026 added Warner Records and Atlantic Recording Corp. as defendants. ([Music Business Worldwide, June 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/musicians-union-sues-umg-and-warner-music-alleging-member-recordings-were-licensed-to-suno-and-udio-without-compensation-or-credit/))
- **Ed Newton-Rex resigned as VP of Audio at Stability AI on 15 November 2023** over the company's fair-use position — the highest-profile insider defection in AI music. His argument is market harm: "generative AI models can clearly be used to create works that compete with the copyrighted works they are trained on." He is explicitly pro-AI; he had shipped Stable Audio trained on licensed AudioSparx data, and went on to found Fairly Trained. ([Resignation post](https://ed.newtonrex.com/blog/why-i-resigned-from-stability-ai); [full op-ed, Music Business Worldwide, 15 Nov 2023](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/why-just-resigned-from-my-job-generative-ai/))
- **Newton-Rex then organised the silent album, released 25 February 2025.** *Is This What We Want?* is 12 tracks of ambient room noise recorded in empty studios, credited to "1,000 UK Artists" (Kate Bush, Damon Albarn, Annie Lennox, Hans Zimmer, Tori Amos; Paul McCartney added a track to the November 2025 vinyl). The track titles spell out: "The British Government must not legalise music theft to benefit AI companies." ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is_This_What_We_Want))
- **Historical precedent #1 — the talkies.** About 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs after sound film arrived in 1927. The AFM founded the Music Defense League in 1930 to mobilise public opinion against "canned music." AFM president Joseph Weber privately conceded the point: "Nothing will destroy the usefulness of an organization surer than to set its face against progress." ([AFM, 125 Years of History](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/))
- **Historical precedent #2 — the drum machine.** The Linn LM-1 launched in 1980 at $5,500; only 525 were ever built, yet "it was feared the LM-1 would put every session drummer in Los Angeles out of work and it caused many of L.A.'s top session drummers (Jeff Porcaro is one example) to purchase their own drum machines and learn to program them themselves in order to stay employed." Roger Linn's own 1985 interview headline read "I murdered the drummer!" Note: the LM-1's sounds were *sampled from working session drummers* — the displaced supplied the weapon. ([Linn LM-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linn_LM-1); [Drum machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_machine))
- **Historical precedent #3 — sampling and the Amen break.** The seven-second break at 1:26 of the Winstons' "Amen, Brother" (1969), played by Gregory Coleman, became the backbone of jungle, drum & bass and countless hip-hop records. Bandleader Richard Spencer only learned of its use in 1996, by which point the statute of limitations had run. Coleman died homeless in 2006. A 2015 GoFundMe raised over £18,000 for Spencer as a voluntary thank-you — never royalties. Spencer died in 2020. ([Wikipedia: Amen break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break))
- **Historical precedent #4 — Auto-Tune.** Andy Hildebrand released it in September 1997; Cher's "Believe" (1998) turned a correction tool into an aesthetic; Jay-Z released "D.O.A. (Death of Auto-Tune)" in 2009; Time listed it among the 50 worst inventions in 2010, with critic Josh Tyrangiel calling it "Photoshop for the human voice." Hildebrand's reply to the "is it evil" question: "Well, my wife wears makeup. Is that evil?" Today it is uncontroversially an instrument. ([Wikipedia: Auto-Tune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto-Tune))
- **The abundance problem is now measurable.** Deezer reported on 21 July 2026 that fully AI-generated tracks exceeded 50% of daily uploads for the first time, averaging about 90,000 tracks a day in June 2026 — up from roughly 10,000/day (10%) in January 2025. Yet AI tracks account for only 1–3% of actual streams, and up to 85% of streams *of* fully AI tracks were fraudulent in 2025. Supply is infinite; attention is not. ([Deezer Newsroom, 21 Jul 2026](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2026/07/ai-music-exceeds-50-percent-daily-uploads-deezer/))
- **Listeners can't tell — and say they care anyway.** In a Deezer/Ipsos survey of 9,000 adults across eight markets (published 12 November 2025), 97% failed a blind test to identify fully AI-generated tracks; 80% still want AI music labelled, 70% think it threatens musicians' livelihoods and 69% think it should be paid less. ([Deezer/Ipsos, 12 Nov 2025](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2025/11/deezer-ipsos-survey-ai-music/))
- **Academic work sharpens the paradox.** Andrew Edelblum and Joshua Poe (University of Dayton), in *Psychology & Marketing* (reported 9 June 2026): 88% of consumers say they prefer human-made music, but within 60 seconds of listening the preference evaporates and they rate AI tracks equally. They remain 15–25% less willing to *endorse* it. Poe: "They like it, they just don't want to be seen liking it." The effect did not replicate for poetry. ([University of Dayton, 9 Jun 2026](https://udayton.edu/blogs/business/2026/26-music-ai-research.php))
- **Which jobs are exposed:** CISAC's December 2024 study projects generative AI will account for roughly **60% of music library revenues** by 2028 versus about **20% of streaming platform revenues** — i.e. production/library music, the invisible bed music of TV, ads and corporate video, is the epicentre. UK Music's November 2025 survey found songwriters and composers most alarmed, and singled out "media and production music" as the area under particular threat. ([CISAC, Dec 2024](https://www.cisac.org/Newsroom/news-releases/global-economic-study-shows-human-creators-future-risk-generative-ai); [UK Music, 27 Nov 2025](https://www.ukmusic.org/news/two-thirds-of-creators-say-ai-poses-a-threat-to-their-careers-uk-music-report-reveals/))
- **Which jobs are not (yet):** a Sonarworks/Sound On Sound survey of 1,100+ working creators (4 February 2026) found producers happily delegate *technical* labour — audio restoration 58%, mixing assistance 38%, mastering 33.9% — but only 20.9% use AI composition tools, and 57.9% still frame AI as "a tool." Their top concern is loss of originality (77%), ahead of job displacement (42%). ([Sonarworks, 4 Feb 2026](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/research/future-music-production-human-producer-survey-2026))
- **Training-data bias is documented, not speculative.** "Music for All" (Mehta et al., arXiv, February 2025, NAACL 2025) found **only 5.7% of the total hours in existing music-generation datasets come from non-Western genres**. Its companion paper "Missing Melodies" (December 2024) found 86% of dataset hours and 93% of researchers focus on Global North music, with Global South genres at 14.6% of data. Practical consequence: models round microtones to the nearest Western equivalent and default to Western tonal/rhythmic structure. ([Music for All](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07328); [Missing Melodies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04100))
- **The parasocial premium is real and priced.** Xania Monet — an AI persona created by Telisha "Nikki" Jones of Mississippi, who writes the lyrics and generates everything else through Suno — signed with Hallwood Media for a reported $3 million in September 2025 and became the first AI act on a Billboard radio airplay chart (#30, Adult R&B Airplay). Kehlani objected that Jones was "doing none of the work." The interesting datum is the counter-move: Nettwerk CEO Terry McBride said he wouldn't sign her because "that's not going to be a touring entity." ([Wikipedia: Xania Monet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xania_Monet))
- **Disclosure is where the industry landed, July 2026.** IFPI, RIAA, A2IM, WIN, IMPALA, the Grammys, SAG-AFTRA and the Human Artistry Campaign announced a voluntary labelling standard on 10 July 2026 distinguishing "AI-Generated" (AI produced the entirety or primary portion of creative elements) from "AI-Assisted" (created substantially by humans). It covers sound recordings only — not lyrics, composition, videos or cover art. ([RIAA, 10 Jul 2026](https://www.riaa.com/music-community-introduces-new-labeling-programto-distinguish-generative-ai-in-sound-recordings/))
- **Can an AI record win a Grammy? Yes if a human did meaningful work; no for the AI itself — and one AI-assisted record has already won.** The Recording Academy's "Generative Artificial Intelligence" rule, announced 16 June 2023 for the 66th Grammys and carried unchanged into the current 69th Grammy Awards rulebook (dated 11 June 2026), reads verbatim: "Only human creators are eligible to be submitted for consideration for, nominated for, or win a Grammy Award. A work that contains no human authorship is not eligible in any categories." ([69th Grammy Awards Rulebook, pp. 8–9](https://ra-grammy-media.ncp.consulting/uploads/2026/06/69_Rulebook_06112026-FINAL2.pdf); [grammy.com Rules & Guidelines](https://www.grammy.com/awards/rules-guidelines/); [CBS News, 17 Jun 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/grammy-rule-artificial-intelligence-only-human-creators-eligible-awards/))
- **The three-part test is the most quotable line-drawing rule in music, and it is category-by-category.** A work containing AI material is eligible if: "(1) the human authorship component of the work submitted must be meaningful and more than de minimis; (2) such human authorship component must be relevant to the category in which such work is entered (e.g., if the work is submitted in a songwriting category, there must be meaningful and more than de minimis human authorship in respect of the music and/or lyrics; if the work is submitted in a performance category, there must be meaningful and more than de minimis human authorship in respect of the performance); and (3) the author(s) of any A.I. material incorporated into the work are not eligible to be nominees or Grammy recipients insofar as their contribution to the portion of the work that consists of such A.I material is concerned." The rulebook even defines the term: "De minimis is defined as lacking significance or importance; so minor as to merit disregard." ([69th Rulebook](https://ra-grammy-media.ncp.consulting/uploads/2026/06/69_Rulebook_06112026-FINAL2.pdf))
- **The Academy kept a discretionary kill switch, and no detection machinery.** The same rulebook's terms of use reserve the right "to disqualify any entry in a particular category, if it determines, in the Academy's sole discretion, that such entry does not incorporate meaningful and more than de minimis human authorship that is relevant to such category." There is no AI-disclosure box, no audit and no detection tool described anywhere in the 69th rulebook — enforcement runs on submitter honesty plus genre-screening committees. The 69th eligibility period is 31 August 2025 – 28 August 2026, nominations 16 November 2026, ceremony 7 February 2027. ([69th Rulebook](https://ra-grammy-media.ncp.consulting/uploads/2026/06/69_Rulebook_06112026-FINAL2.pdf))
- **An AI-assisted track has already won.** The Beatles' "Now and Then" — Lennon's 1977 home cassette de-mixed with "MAL," the machine-learning stem-separation tool built by Peter Jackson's WingNut Films over four years — became the first AI-assisted recording nominated for a Grammy (Record of the Year) and won **Best Rock Performance at the 67th Grammys on 2 February 2025**. McCartney's framing is the whole defence of the rule: "nothing has been artificially or synthetically created. It's all real and we all play on it. We cleaned up some existing recordings." ([Now and Then](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Now_and_Then_(Beatles_song)); [67th Annual Grammy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67th_Annual_Grammy_Awards))
- **The rule answers the Xania Monet question cleanly, and Harvey Mason jr. has said so out loud.** A record whose lyrics are written by Telisha "Nikki" Jones but whose vocal is synthesised through Suno has meaningful human authorship in the *songwriting* categories and effectively none in the *performance* ones. The Academy's CEO, 5 February 2026: "You can submit a song that has used AI," but "what we're not going to do is give a Grammy to an AI artist for singing" — and on voice cloning, "That's not even debatable. No cloning." He also insists "there's a place for AI, and it is a tool," and that he has "seen AI in every studio, in every session." ([Music Business Worldwide, 5 Feb 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/harvey-mason-jr-on-ai-grammy-eligibility-and-why-human-creativity-will-always-matter/))
- **Mason's one-line version, 8 December 2025:** "Using AI does not make your entry ineligible. It just makes you have to choose the right categories to be considered in." He calls the AI question "the toughest part of my job." Note the procedural detail that makes the rule bite less than it sounds: artists nominate their own category, and a screening committee needs a 66.66% supermajority to move an entry out of it. ([Billboard, 8 Dec 2025](https://billboard.substack.com/p/how-grammys-boss-harvey-mason-jr))
- **The protocol has never actually been tested, because the first case died on a rights issue instead.** Mason initially suggested the Drake/Weeknd soundalike "Heart on My Sleeve" could be eligible in songwriting categories since a human wrote it; on 11 September 2023 he ruled it out — but on clearance grounds, not AI grounds: "the vocals were not legally obtained, the vocals were not cleared by the label or the artists, and the song is not commercially available and, because of that, it's not eligible." ([Complete Music Update, 11 Sep 2023](https://completemusicupdate.com/controversial-ghostwriter-track-not-eligible-for-grammy-consideration/))
- **The strongest critique: "meaningful" is undefined exactly where it matters.** Mark Benincosa (West Virginia University), a recording engineer, in The Conversation on 30 January 2026: generate the hook — melody and lyrics — with one prompt, then have humans write the verses and bridge around it. "If AI generates the catchy part first, does that mean it is ultimately responsible for the other sections created by a human?" He treats AI backing vocals and AI-added drum swing as clearly fine, and the AI-generated hook as the case nobody can adjudicate. ([The Conversation, 30 Jan 2026](https://theconversation.com/grammys-ai-rules-aim-to-keep-music-human-but-large-gray-area-leaves-questions-about-authenticity-and-authorship-274504))
- **The ghost-artist precedent predates AI and matters more than people think.** Liz Pelly's reporting (Harper's, December 2024; book *Mood Machine*, 7 January 2025) described Spotify's internal "Perfect Fit Content" programme, commissioning cheap functional music for mood playlists at better margins. Sweden's Dagens Nyheter found in 2022 that ~20 musicians were behind 830 fake artist names, 495 of them placed on Spotify's curated playlists, with one producer, Johan Röhr, credited on 2,700+ songs. Spotify: "We do not and have never created 'fake' artists and put them on Spotify playlists." The point for the panel: the *demand* for authorless background music was manufactured before AI could supply it. ([Wikipedia summary with sourcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controversy_over_fake_artists_on_Spotify))
- **The Velvet Sundown, July 2025:** an entirely AI-generated "band" passed one million Spotify monthly listeners before being exposed; a purported spokesperson called it an "art hoax" made with Suno, then denied involvement. Spotify's bio now describes it as "a synthetic music project guided by human creative direction." It is the best single case study of listeners liking the audio and revolting at the disclosure. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Velvet_Sundown))

## Contested / two-sided

**1. Does a song without an intending author mean anything?**
*Claim:* No. Cave: songs "arise out of suffering... the complex, internal human struggle of creation"; Gioia: "AI does not possess a self... any art it creates will inevitably feel empty and hollow"; Eno: emotional impact is "not possible unless you assume intentionality on the part of something being made."
*Counter:* The Dayton study is the strongest empirical rebuttal — 88% say they prefer human music, and the preference vanishes within a minute of actual listening. If meaning were audible, blind tests wouldn't fail at 97%. Herndon's position is that authorship was always distributed anyway: "Creativity happens in swarm, in community." Also note the *category* objection: instrumental library music, film underscore and functional ambient have never carried authorial intention as their value proposition.
*Sharpest version of the tension:* intention may not be *in* the audio at all — it may be a claim about the listener's belief state, which makes labelling, not sound quality, the whole ballgame.

**2. Is this just the player piano / drum machine / Auto-Tune panic again?**
*Claim (continuity):* Every displacement was survived. AFM's own president said in the 1930s that setting "its face against progress" would destroy the union. Auto-Tune went from Time's 50 worst inventions (2010) to a standard instrument. The Linn LM-1 sold 525 units and drummers who learned to program it kept working.
*Counter (discontinuity):* Three differences worth stating. (a) Prior technologies replaced a *task*; generative models are trained on the specific outputs of the people they displace — the Amen break problem at industrial scale. (b) Scale: 90,000 AI tracks/day on one platform, versus 525 LM-1s ever built. (c) The drum machine still required someone to program it and someone to sell the record; a prompt collapses the entire chain. Also: the popular story that the UK Musicians' Union formally *banned* synthesizers in the early 1980s appears to be a myth — scholarship indicates it was raised at a single branch meeting and never became policy, so be careful using it as precedent. **[UNVERIFIED — the scholarly article making this correction (Williamson & Cloonan-adjacent work in *Social History*, 2016) was inaccessible; treat the "synth ban" as folklore until confirmed.]**

**3. Democratization or devaluation?**
*Claim:* Suno's mission is to let people who lack training, time or money make music; Shulman argues "the majority of people don't enjoy the majority of the time they spend making music" and that access should scale to a billion people. Xania Monet is the concrete case: a woman in Olive Branch, Mississippi with poems and no studio budget reaching a Billboard airplay chart.
*Counter:* Newton-Rex — a generative-AI lifer, not a Luddite — argues the models compete with the very works they were trained on, so "democratization" redistributes from many small creators to a few platforms. Krukowski goes further and says copyright is the wrong frame entirely: musicians "need labor rights more than copyrights," and the AFM's June 2026 suit against *the labels* rather than the AI firms is that thesis in litigation.
*Uncomfortable fact for both sides:* the Deezer data shows AI tracks are >50% of uploads but 1–3% of streams, with 85% of their streams fraudulent. So far this is less a democratization of audiences than a democratization of uploading.

**4. Whose music is in the model?**
*Claim:* Generative music is a homogenization engine — 5.7% non-Western training hours means the models literally cannot represent most of the world's music, and will round maqam microtones to the nearest Western semitone. Herndon: "a corporate recreation of subcultures that we can consume and never pay attribution to."
*Counter:* This is a pre-existing industry bias that AI inherits rather than invents — the recorded-music economy was already ~90% Global North by catalogue and capital. And the same papers show parameter-efficient fine-tuning improves Hindustani classical and Turkish makam performance with adapters at 0.1% of model parameters, so the gap is fixable in principle. The counter-counter: those gains degraded Western-genre performance, and the authors conclude the *dataset* imbalance, not the fine-tuning, is the thing that has to change.

**5. If supply is infinite, what becomes scarce?**
*Claim:* Curation, taste and provenance. Shulman himself says "taste is the only thing that matters in art and skill is going to matter a lot less." Labels and streamers are converging on labelling (RIAA et al., July 2026) precisely because provenance is now the scarce, priceable good.
*Counter:* Scarcity migrates to the body and the room — live performance, residencies, physical objects — not to taste. Nettwerk's Terry McBride's objection to signing an AI act was purely operational: "that's not going to be a touring entity." **[UNVERIFIED — 2025 Pollstar figures circulating suggest the Top 100 tours grossed ~$8.9bn, down from a record ~$9.5bn in 2024, while average tickets sold per show hit an all-time high; I could not fetch Pollstar directly, so treat the exact numbers as unconfirmed.]**
*Third position worth raising:* neither. Pelly's Perfect Fit Content reporting suggests a large share of listening was never about taste *or* the body — it is functional background audio, where the authorless product is the point, and where AI simply lowers the cost of something Spotify was already commissioning from humans in 2017.

**6. Does the Grammy rule draw a workable line, or just relocate the argument?**
*Claim (it works):* It is the only authorship test in music that is written down, category-specific and already applied. It has a defined threshold ("meaningful and more than de minimis," with de minimis itself defined in the rulebook), it splits songwriting from performance so the Xania Monet case resolves in one sentence, and it produced a real outcome — the Beatles won Best Rock Performance in 2025 for a record built with machine-learning stem separation, while Mason says flatly that no AI voice gets a performance Grammy. Compare the US Copyright Office and the JASRAC/NexTone registration questions, which are still arguing about the same threshold with far less operational detail.
*Counter (it doesn't):* Nothing in the 69th rulebook requires a submitter to disclose AI use at all, there is no detection mechanism, and Benincosa's hook problem — prompt the chorus, hand-write the verses — falls in a gap the words "meaningful" and "relevant to the category" cannot close. The rule has also never been stress-tested: its only celebrated test case, "Heart on My Sleeve," was disposed of on clearance and commercial-availability grounds before the AI question was reached. And the Academy's escape hatch is its "sole discretion," which is not a test, it is a veto.
*The awkward asymmetry to put to a panel:* the Grammys signed the 10 July 2026 labelling standard, which classifies *recordings* as AI-Generated or AI-Assisted, but their own entry process asks entrants for no such declaration. The trade body wants disclosure at retail and runs its own trophy on the honour system.

## Panel-ready questions

1. **Ted Gioia says AI art "will inevitably feel empty and hollow," but 97% of listeners in a nine-thousand-person blind test couldn't tell the difference, and the Dayton study found the stated preference for human music collapses within sixty seconds. If the hollowness isn't audible, is it real — or is it a belief we're protecting with a label?**
2. **The AFM lost 22,000 theatre jobs to sound film in 1927 and fought it; in 2026 it sued Universal and Warner over AI licensing. Which of those is the better analogy for what should happen now — and is there any version of this where the union wins something more durable than a rate card?**
3. **CISAC projects AI will take about 60% of music library revenue but only 20% of streaming revenue. Is it honest to talk about "AI killing music" when the exposure is overwhelmingly concentrated in the anonymous, work-for-hire end — jingles, beds, demo vocals, production libraries — that the industry has always treated as disposable?**
4. **Only 5.7% of the hours in music-AI training data are non-Western. Is the right response to get more of the world's music *into* the models — which means more scraping of communities with the least legal power — or to keep it *out* and accept that these tools will only ever speak Western?**
5. **Nick Cave says the songwriter using ChatGPT should "desist if he wants to continue calling himself a songwriter." Telisha Jones writes every lyric for Xania Monet and generates the rest, and got a $3 million deal. Where exactly is the line between an instrument and a ghostwriter — and would any of you accept a rule you'd be willing to apply to Auto-Tune, sampling and session-player uncredited co-writes retroactively?**
6. **The Grammys already gave Best Rock Performance to a record whose lead vocal was extracted by machine learning from a 1977 cassette, and their rulebook says an AI-voiced track can still win in a songwriting category as long as a human wrote the words. So on the Academy's own test, Xania Monet's lyricist is Grammy-eligible and Xania Monet is not. Is that the right line — and who in this room would tell the audience the trophy belongs to the person who never sang a note?**

## Open questions

- **Is there any hard number for session-musician work actually lost to AI?** Everything available is either macro-projection (CISAC's 24%), sentiment (UK Music's 66%), or anecdote. What would settle it: AFM Local 47 and Local 802 recording-session data 2019–2026, or PRS/MU membership income breakdowns by role. Note the same gap existed for the drum machine — the 1980s claim has never been quantified either.
- **The Musicians' Union "synthesizer ban."** Widely repeated, apparently false, but I could not open the scholarly correction (paywalled/403). Settling it needs the full text of the *Social History* article on needletime and the MU, or MU Executive Committee minutes from 1980–83. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- **Liz Pelly's Harper's essay "The Ghosts in the Machine" (December 2024) is behind a hard paywall** (HTTP 402), so the PFC claims here are cited via secondary summaries and the Dagens Nyheter numbers. The book *Mood Machine* (Atria, 7 January 2025) is the primary source to quote from if the panel goes deep on ghost artists.
- **Did anyone actually benefit from "democratization"?** Xania Monet is one case; Suno claims very large user numbers (figures around 100 million total users and a ~$5.4bn valuation circulate for 2026 but I could not verify them from a primary source — **[UNVERIFIED]**). What's missing is any study of *who* the new creators are by income, geography or disability status. Absent that, the democratization case rests on a vendor's mission statement.
- **Has anything AI-generated actually been *submitted* to the Grammys and screened out?** The Academy publishes entry totals but not disqualifications, and I found no source — including the Xania Monet coverage — confirming she was entered for the 68th Grammys (nominations 7 November 2025, ceremony 1 February 2026) or that any entry has ever been rejected under the de minimis rule. **[UNVERIFIED]** What would settle it: Recording Academy screening-committee data, or a statement from Hallwood Media about the 69th entry window (7 July – 21 August 2026).
- **Whether "meaningful and more than de minimis" has ever been given a worked example by the Academy.** The rulebook defines *de minimis* but not *meaningful*, and Harvey Mason jr.'s public statements are principles, not tests. What would settle it: the Academy's internal screening guidance, or a published appeal decision. Until then the rule is a standard, not a bright line, and Benincosa's AI-hook case is genuinely unanswered.
- **Whether the AFM's Article 21 "new use" theory survives.** Warner has argued the clause cannot operate because no AFM standard AI agreement exists. A ruling either way is the single most consequential labour outcome pending, and it was still live as of the July 2026 amended complaint.
- **No verified primary source found for the often-quoted $500,000 Music Defense League ad budget (1930)** or for Roger Linn's full "I murdered the drummer" interview (International Musician and Recording World, 1985). Both are cited secondhand everywhere; the AFM archive and IMRW back issues would settle them. **[UNVERIFIED]**
