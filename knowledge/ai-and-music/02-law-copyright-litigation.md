# Law, copyright and litigation

**TL;DR for the assistant:** Two days ago, on 31 July 2026, a Munich court handed down the first European ruling that training a music AI on copyrighted songs requires a licence — GEMA beat Suno, and the court even applied US fair use law to Suno's US training and found all four factors against it. In the United States, by contrast, no court has yet ruled on fair use in the music cases: the record labels sued Suno and Udio in June 2024, Warner and Universal have since settled and signed licensing deals, Sony is still litigating, and on 15 July 2026 a hacker leaked Suno's own source code, which names YouTube Music, Deezer and Genius as training sources — so provenance is no longer something the labels have to infer. The precedent that does exist is contradictory — Judge Alsup called AI training "exceedingly transformative" in the Anthropic case, then Anthropic paid $1.5 billion for the pirated books it used; Judge Chhabria let Meta win but said the plaintiffs "made the wrong arguments" and handed rightsholders a roadmap called market dilution. Meanwhile the US Copyright Office says prompts alone don't make you an author, so a purely AI-generated track has no US copyright at all. And voice — the thing artists actually care about — is still barely protected federally: the NO FAKES Act cleared Senate committee in June 2026 but has not passed.

## Key facts

### The core music cases: labels v. Suno and Udio

- **24 June 2024** — Sony Music, UMG Recordings and Warner Records, coordinated by the RIAA, filed copyright suits against **Suno** (D. Mass.) and **Uncharted Labs / Udio** (S.D.N.Y.), alleging mass unlicensed copying of sound recordings to train music generators. ([RIAA announcement + both complaints](https://www.riaa.com/record-companies-bring-landmark-cases-for-responsible-ai-againstsuno-and-udio-in-boston-and-new-york-federal-courts-respectively/); [Suno complaint PDF](https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Suno-complaint-file-stamped20.pdf))
- **August 2024** — Suno's answer conceded it trained on copyrighted recordings and asserted **fair use**. That concession is why the case is unusually clean: liability turns almost entirely on fair use, not on whether copying happened.
- **29 October 2025** — **UMG settled with Udio** and signed recorded-music and publishing licences for a new "walled garden" AI platform launching in 2026, trained only on authorised music, with participating artists opting in. Udio's existing product stayed live but with fingerprinting and filtering, and downloads were switched off — which infuriated existing users. ([UMG/Udio press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/universal-music-group-and-udio-announce-udios-first-strategic-agreements-for-new-licensed-ai-music-creation-platform-302599129.html); [MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/universal-music-settles-udio-lawsuit-strikes-deal-for-licensed-ai-music-platform/))
- **25 November 2025** — **Warner Music settled with Suno**, the first major to do so, and simultaneously *sold Songkick to Suno*. Terms: licensed models in 2026 with current models deprecated; downloads restricted to paid tiers with monthly caps; artist and songwriter **opt-in** for name, image, likeness, voice and compositions. ([MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/warner-music-group-settles-with-suno-strikes-first-of-its-kind-deal-with-ai-song-generator/); [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/suno-warner-music-group-ai-music-settlement-lawsuit-1235472868/))
- **As of mid-2026, Sony Music has settled with neither Suno nor Udio, and UMG has not settled with Suno.** UMG–Suno talks reportedly stalled in April 2026. The live US case is *UMG + Sony v. Suno* in Massachusetts before Chief Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV.
- **21 May 2026** — UMG and Sony moved to expand the Suno case from roughly 560 works to **61,026 recordings**, identified by running Audible Magic fingerprinting against Suno's training data. Suno opposed, arguing the expansion would delay a fair-use ruling. Judge Saylor had not ruled as of late July 2026. ([MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-asks-court-to-block-umg-and-sony-from-expanding-copyright-lawsuit-to-over-61000-recordings/))
- **15 July 2026 — Suno's source code leaked, and it lists the scraping sources by name.** A hacker who breached Suno in a **November 2025 supply-chain attack** using stolen employee credentials gave 2023–24 internal source code and documentation to **404 Media**. The code enumerates the training corpus by source string — `genius_hq, youtube_music, freesound, jamendo, imp, deezer, ytm_tagged` — with dataset sizes: **2,013,545 music clips / 113,879 hours from YouTube Music** plus 152,162 hours tagged `ytm_tagged`, **62,117 hours from Pond5**, **17,615 hours from Genius**, **12,287 hours from Deezer**, plus Jamendo, Freesound and the International Music Score Library Project, and roughly **420,000 podcasts** pulled via PodcastIndex RSS (~1 million hours attempted). Other code shows Suno searching YouTube for a cappella versions and routing scrapes through **Bright Data** proxies. ([404 Media, the primary report](https://www.404media.co/hack-reveals-suno-ai-music-generator-scraped-youtube-deezer-and-genius/); [TechCrunch, 15 Jul 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/15/hack-suggests-ai-music-generator-suno-scraped-youtube-for-training-data/))
  - **Suno's response** (16 July 2026): its models "have been trained on publicly available music files" from "third-party websites on the open Internet," and the leaked material is "outdated source code that is no longer in use." Note what Suno is *not* denying — it has conceded since August 2024 that it trained on copyrighted recordings. The dispute is legality, not fact. ([MBW, 16 Jul 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-scraped-youtube-deezer-and-genius-to-train-its-ai-hacked-code-reveals/))
  - **Why this is the most consequential fact in the US case.** MBW's framing: it "potentially strengthen[s] UMG and Sony's legal case." Three reasons. (1) It corroborates the RIAA's **September 2025 stream-ripping allegation** — circumventing YouTube's "rolling cipher" is a **DMCA §1201 anti-circumvention** claim that runs *independently of fair use*; you can win on fair use and still lose on §1201. (2) The Munich court on 31 July 2026 already treated the YouTube stream-ripping as **undisputed** (see below). (3) It converts provenance from an **inference** into documentary evidence: UMG and Sony's 61,026-work expansion motion rested on running Audible Magic fingerprinting *against* Suno's training data; the leaked manifest names the sources directly, which is exactly the kind of showing that makes an expansion motion easier for Judge Saylor to grant.
  - **The Anthropic parallel is the line to draw on stage.** Judge Alsup split the question: training was "exceedingly transformative" fair use, but **acquiring** the corpus by piracy was not — and that half cost Anthropic **$1.5 billion**. If the leaked code shows Suno's corpus was assembled by circumventing YouTube's technical protections rather than licensed or lawfully obtained, Suno's exposure sits on the *Anthropic* side of that line even if a court agrees with it on transformativeness.
  - **Awkward detail for a music-industry room:** **Deezer** appears in the leaked manifest as a scraping victim — the same Deezer whose AI-detection tooling the industry now relies on to flag AI tracks.
- **20–22 July 2026 — the same intrusion also exposed users.** **55.3 million** Suno user records were added to **Have I Been Pwned** on 20 July 2026: email addresses, names, phone numbers, physical addresses, purchase amounts and partial card data (type, expiry, last four) from Stripe. Suno confirmed a "limited security incident that was quickly contained" in November 2025, said it "primarily involved outdated source code," and concluded individual breach notifications "were not warranted under applicable privacy laws" — i.e. customers were never told at the time. This is a **privacy/security story, not a copyright story**; keep them separate on stage, but note they are the *same* November 2025 breach. ([Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/Breach/Suno); [MBW, 22 Jul 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-admits-data-breach-that-reportedly-leaked-55-3-million-users-data/))
- In the parallel Udio case, **Judge Hellerstein (SDNY) refused Sony's bid to add 30,442 recordings**, keeping that case at the 333 works originally pleaded — but he added that plaintiffs "have the right to seek to stop infringement of, and recover damages for, all copyrighted works" and that "there is no requirement that it be done in [that] lawsuit." Sony took the hint.
- **20 July 2026 — Sony filed a *second*, standalone suit against Udio in SDNY asserting 30,117 sound recordings**, quoting Hellerstein's language back at the court. The complaint says the 30,117 works are "only a small portion" of the hundreds of thousands of infringing recordings Sony identified in discovery in the first case. Sony is now the only major litigating against both Suno and Udio, on two fronts against Udio. ([MBW, 20 Jul 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/sony-music-files-new-lawsuit-against-ai-platform-udio-asserting-over-30000-sound-recordings-a-judge-barred-it-from-adding-to-its-original-case/))
- Suno is fighting to keep the **Warner settlement contract sealed**; a magistrate sided with Suno in April 2026. UMG and Sony want it because a functioning licensing market is direct evidence on fair use factor four. ([MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-fights-to-keep-warner-music-settlement-terms-away-from-umg-and-sony/))
- **June–July 2026** — the **American Federation of Musicians sued UMG and Warner**, alleging the Suno/Udio settlements licensed members' recordings without triggering the "new use" payments their collective bargaining agreements require. The labels deny the obligation. ([MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-musicians-union-files-amended-lawsuit-against-universal-and-warner-over-suno-and-udio-ai-deals/))

### Germany: the two rulings that actually decided something

- **11 November 2025 — GEMA v. OpenAI**, Munich Regional Court I, case 42 O 14139/24. First European court to hold an AI developer directly liable. Held: **memorisation of lyrics inside model parameters is a reproduction** under §16 UrhG (the court analogised to lossy MP3 compression); the §44b text-and-data-mining exception covers building the training corpus but **not the training of the model itself**; OpenAI, not users, is liable. Nine German songs including "Atemlos durch die Nacht" and "Über den Wolken". Penalties up to €250,000 per violation. OpenAI is appealing to the Munich Higher Regional Court. ([Norton Rose Fulbright analysis](https://www.insidetechlaw.com/blog/2025/11/germany-delivers-landmark-copyright-ruling-against-openai-what-it-means-for-ai-and-ip))
- **31 July 2026 — GEMA v. Suno**, Munich Regional Court I, case **42 O 763/25**. The single most important music-AI ruling to date. Six compositions: "Atemlos durch die Nacht", "Rasputin", "Big in Japan", "Forever Young", the chorus of "Mambo No. 5", "Daddy Cool". Lyrics were *not* at issue — this was about melody, harmony, rhythm. ([Court press release 16/2026, primary source](https://www.justiz.bayern.de/gerichte-und-behoerden/landgericht/muenchen-1/presse/2026/16.php); [GEMA statement](https://www.gema.de/de/w/suno-entscheidung-2026); [Variety](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/suno-loses-ai-lawsuit-gema-1236825010/))
  - The court found the works **reproducibly present in Suno models v3.5 and v4**, which were stored on servers in Germany.
  - **Suno, not the user, is responsible**: the prompts supplied only the lyrics, a style tag and a title — *no melody, harmony, rhythm or arrangement* — so the model determined the musical content.
  - **Merely offering the generator** was itself held to infringe the unnamed right of communication to the public under §15(2) UrhG.
  - It took **international jurisdiction over the US training** under §131 VGG (a special forum for collecting societies), applied US law under the country-of-protection principle, and held **all four fair use factors under 17 U.S.C. §107 weigh against Suno**, applying the Supreme Court's *Warhol* framework.
  - It expressly **distinguished *Bartz* and *Kadrey***: in those cases the training data was not substantially reproduced in outputs; here it was.
  - Undisputed: Suno used **stream-ripping from YouTube, circumventing YouTube's "rolling cipher"** technical protection measure. Two weeks earlier, the leaked source code independently documented YouTube Music as the single largest source in Suno's corpus — the German finding and the American leak now point the same way.
  - Remedies: injunction, disclosure of revenue, damages to be quantified. **The judgment is not final**; Suno says it rests on "a fundamental mischaracterization" of the technology and is weighing appeal.

### The US fair-use precedent that music courts will borrow from

- **Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence** (D. Del., 11 Feb 2025, Judge Bibas): at least 2,243 Westlaw headnotes were copyrightable and copying them to build a competing legal-research tool was **not** fair use; market harm included the market for licensing content as AI training data. On interlocutory appeal, the **Third Circuit heard argument on 11 June 2026** (Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves, Bove) and **has not yet ruled** — it will be the first federal appellate word on AI training and fair use. ([LawSites argument report](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/06/at-3rd-circuit-judges-press-ross-and-thomson-reuters-on-fair-use-ai-training-and-market-harm.html))
- **Bartz v. Anthropic** (N.D. Cal., 23 June 2025, Judge Alsup): training LLMs on books was **"exceedingly transformative"** fair use; buying and scanning print books was fair use; **retaining 7M+ pirated books in a permanent "central library" was not.** ([Order, Dkt. 231](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0.pdf))
- Anthropic then settled the piracy class for **$1.5 billion** — roughly $3,000 per work across ~500,000 works — plus destruction of the pirated copies. Preliminary approval was denied 8 Sept 2025 and granted 25 Sept 2025; final approval came in 2026 from Judge Martínez-Olguín, with over 91% of eligible authors filing claims. It is the largest copyright settlement in history. The release covers **past training only, not outputs**. ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/judge-approves-record-1-5-billion-settlement-involving-anthropic/); [Authors Guild explainer](https://authorsguild.org/advocacy/artificial-intelligence/what-authors-need-to-know-about-the-anthropic-settlement/))
- **Kadrey v. Meta** (N.D. Cal., 25 June 2025, Judge Chhabria): summary judgment **for Meta** — but the opinion is a warning shot. Chhabria wrote the ruling "stands only for the proposition that these plaintiffs made the wrong arguments and failed to develop a record in support of the right one," and named **market dilution** as the winning theory: a model that can generate "literally millions of secondary works" can flood and depress the market for originals even without producing infringing copies. He explicitly rejected Alsup's schoolchildren analogy as "inapt." ([Order, Dkt. 598](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.415175/gov.uscourts.cand.415175.598.0.pdf))
- **NYT v. OpenAI & Microsoft** (S.D.N.Y. 1:23-cv-11195, filed 27 Dec 2023, Judge Stein; now in MDL 1:25-md-03143): motions to dismiss largely denied in spring 2025; a **20-million ChatGPT conversation sample** ordered produced in Nov 2025 and affirmed 5 Jan 2026; summary judgment briefing closed 2 April 2026. On **9 July 2026** the news plaintiffs moved for **sanctions**, including an adverse-inference instruction, alleging OpenAI misrepresented its ability to search its own systems and deleted or made unsearchable billions of conversations. No merits ruling, no settlement.
- **Getty Images v. Stability AI** [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), 4 Nov 2025, Mrs Justice Joanna Smith: Getty **abandoned** its primary training claims mid-trial (no evidence training happened in the UK); the secondary-infringement claim failed because Stable Diffusion's **weights "at no point contained copies"** of Getty's works. Only a "historic and extremely limited" trade mark win over watermarks. ([Judgment PDF](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Getty-Images-v-Stability-AI.pdf)) — This is in **direct technical tension** with Munich's memorisation reasoning.

### Copyrightability of AI output

- **US Copyright Office, Part 2: Copyrightability, 29 January 2025.** Conclusion: existing doctrine is adequate and no new legislation is needed; and — the quotable line — *"prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the authors of the output."* Human-authored expression that is **perceptible in the output**, plus creative selection, coordination and arrangement, remains protectable. ([Report PDF](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf))
- **Part 1: Digital Replicas, 31 July 2024** recommended a federal digital-replica law. **Part 3: Generative AI Training, 9 May 2025** is *still a "pre-publication version"* as of August 2026: it holds that dataset compilation clearly implicates the reproduction right, rejects the claim that training is inherently transformative, reads factor four broadly to include dilution, and urges voluntary and collective licensing rather than a compulsory licence. ([Copyright Office AI hub](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/); [Part 3 PDF](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf))
- Register of Copyrights **Shira Perlmutter was dismissed the day after Part 3 was released** (10 May 2025) and sued over her removal. Whether Part 3 ever becomes the Office's final position is genuinely open.
- **Thaler v. Perlmutter**, 130 F.4th 1039 (D.C. Cir., 18 March 2025), Judge Millett: **human authorship is required as a matter of statutory law**; a machine cannot be an author. The court did *not* decide whether the Constitution requires it, nor whether Thaler is the author by virtue of building the machine (waived). **Cert denied 2 March 2026.** ([Opinion PDF](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf); [cert denial](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case))
- **Practical music consequence:** a track generated end-to-end by Suno with a one-line prompt has **no US copyright**. Anyone can copy it. Add human lyrics, arrangement, performance or a substantive edit, and you own that human contribution — not the machine layer.

### Voice, likeness and the deepfake gap

- **ELVIS Act** — Tennessee Public Chapter 588, signed 21 March 2024, effective 1 July 2024. It renamed the 1984 Personal Rights Protection Act the **"Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security Act of 2024"** and added **voice** as a protected attribute. It creates a private right of action, keeps Class A misdemeanor liability, and — the novel bit — allows suit against anyone who **makes available a tool whose primary purpose is producing unauthorised voice replicas**. Record labels can enforce on an artist's behalf. ([Public Chapter 588 PDF](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/acts/113/pub/pc0588.pdf))
- **NO FAKES Act** — reintroduced 20 May 2026 as **S.4591 / H.R.8915**, the fourth iteration since 2023. Would create a federal digital-replica right in voice and visual likeness with DMCA-style notice-and-takedown *plus* a new counter-notification process, and a carve-out for nonprofit libraries, archives and schools. It **preempts state digital-replica laws except causes of action existing as of 2 January 2025**. **Ordered reported out of Senate Judiciary 18 June 2026**; ~15 bipartisan cosponsors; GovTrack put enactment odds at 27%. **It is not law.** ([GovTrack S.4591](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/s4591); [Manatt on the 2026 changes](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/congress-reintroduces-the-no-fakes-act-what-s-new-in-the-2026-bill))
- **"Heart on My Sleeve"** (Ghostwriter977, April 2023) is the cleanest illustration of the gap. UMG got it pulled fast — but reportedly on the basis of a **two-second Metro Boomin producer tag** UMG owned, not the cloned Drake and Weeknd voices. Voice is protected by state right of publicity, and **the DMCA notice-and-takedown regime does not apply to publicity rights**. Without that incidental tag, there was no fast remedy. ([Harvard Law School explainer](https://hls.harvard.edu/today/ai-created-a-song-mimicking-the-work-of-drake-and-the-weeknd-what-does-that-mean-for-copyright-law/))
- **Grimes / elf.tech** (launched 30 April 2023) is the opt-in counter-model: use her official GrimesAI-1 voiceprint free, and if you release commercially, **50% master royalty split**, GrimesAI credited as an artist, her approval before distribution, distributed through TuneCore. 15,000 voice transformations in the first days. ([Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/business/tech/ai-grimes-songs-distributed-tunecore-1235351841/))
- **Denmark** proposed on 26 June 2025 to amend its Copyright Act to give every person a copyright-style right in their **face, body and voice**, lasting **50 years after death**, with explicit carve-outs for parody, satire and caricature, and specific protection for performers against synthetic imitations. Entry into force had been expected 31 March 2026 but slipped after a snap election, and the **European Commission has objected** that this is not copyright in the proper sense — copyright protects an author's own intellectual creation and vests in the creator, not the depicted person. Still pending. ([Verfassungsblog analysis](https://verfassungsblog.de/copyrighting-voice-and-image/); [European Parliament briefing](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_ATA(2026)782611))

### Transparency, training-data disclosure and provenance

- **EU AI Act Article 53** requires GPAI providers to have a copyright policy and publish a **"sufficiently detailed summary" of training content**. The Commission adopted the mandatory template on **24 July 2025**; it requires listing large datasets individually, flagging commercially licensed content, and naming the **top 10% of scraped domain names** (top 5% or 1,000 domains for SMEs), covering all training stages including post-training, with updates every six months. ([Commission announcement](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-presents-template-general-purpose-ai-model-providers-summarise-data-used-train-their); [Article 53 text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/53/); [GPAI guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-gpai-providers))
- **The dates matter:** obligations applied from **2 August 2025**; the Commission's **enforcement powers, including fines up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover, begin 2 August 2026** — today. Models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 have until **2 August 2027**.
- **Digital Omnibus on AI** (proposed 19 Nov 2025; Parliament endorsed 16 June 2026; Council final green light 29 June 2026) delayed high-risk obligations to **2 Dec 2027** (Annex III) and **2 Aug 2028** (Annex I) — but **did not delay Article 50 transparency**, which still applies from 2 August 2026. Only the Art. 50(2) machine-readable marking duty for legacy systems moved, to 2 December 2026. A new prohibition on AI systems generating non-consensual intimate imagery and CSAM starts 2 December 2026, at the top penalty tier (€35M / 7%). ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/); [Parliament press release](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20260316IPR38219/meps-support-postponement-of-certain-rules-on-artificial-intelligence))
- **Japan, Article 30-4** (in force 1 Jan 2019) is the most permissive major-economy TDM rule: works may be exploited for information analysis **including commercially**, provided the purpose is not to "enjoy" the expression. Two big limits: the exception fails where an enjoyment purpose **co-exists** — the Agency for Cultural Affairs specifically flags intentional over-learning, RAG over a database of existing works, and LoRA fine-tuning on a handful of one creator's works to reproduce their expression — and the proviso strips the exception where use **"unreasonably prejudices"** the rightsholder, e.g. copying a commercially sold analysis-ready database or defeating technical anti-training measures. Output-side infringement (similarity + dependence) is untouched. ([ACA "General Understanding on AI and Copyright in Japan", 15 March 2024, English overview](https://www.bunka.go.jp/english/policy/copyright/pdf/94055801_01.pdf))
- **China** made labelling mandatory first. The **Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content**, plus mandatory national standard **GB 45438-2025**, took effect **1 September 2025**: *explicit* visible labels for categories likely to confuse — synthetic voice, face generation and face swap, AI chat, AI writing — and *implicit* metadata labels in **all** AI-generated content. Note the nuance: **digital watermarking is recommended, not compelled**; the metadata field is what is mandatory. Platforms must inspect metadata, add their own identifiers, and retain logs for at least six months. ([Bird & Bird comparison with the EU AI Act](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/new-ai-content-labelling-rules-in-china-what-are-they-and-how-do-they-compare-to-the-eu-ai-act); [translated text](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/ai-labeling/))
- **California AB 853** (Chapter 674, signed 13 October 2025) amends the California AI Transparency Act. Its operative date was pushed from 1 Jan 2026 to **2 August 2026 to align with the EU**. From **1 January 2027**, large online platforms (>2 million monthly users) must **detect standards-compliant provenance data, surface it in the UI, and must not knowingly strip it**; GenAI hosting platforms may not distribute models that cannot carry the required disclosures. Capture-device manufacturers are covered from 1 January 2028. Penalties: $5,000 per violation. ([Bill text](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB853); [Troutman summary](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/10/california-ai-transparency-act-amendments-signed-into-law/))
- The statute is deliberately **standards-agnostic** — it says "widely adopted specifications adopted by an established standards-setting body" and never names **C2PA**. In practice C2PA Content Credentials is the assumed target, and Google's **SynthID** is the leading watermark for AI audio, image and text. **Neither is legally mandated by name anywhere in the world.** ([C2PA 2.1 spec](https://c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/index.html); [SynthID](https://deepmind.google/technologies/synthid/))
- **UK:** the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (Royal Assent 19 June 2025) created no new copyright rules — it forced a process. The statutory report landed **18 March 2026** and dropped the broad TDM-exception-with-opt-out as the government's preferred option, partly because no technical standard exists to express an opt-out at the level of an individual work. In the consultation, **88% of respondents wanted licences required in all cases; only 3% backed the government's original opt-out plan.** The government also signalled it intends to end copyright for purely computer-generated works. ([GOV.UK report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/report-and-impact-assessment-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/report-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence))

## Contested / two-sided

**1. Is training a music model on copyrighted recordings fair use?**
*Yes* — Suno, Udio and the broader AI industry, backed by the reasoning in *Bartz* ("exceedingly transformative") and *Kadrey*: the model learns statistical relationships, not expression, and outputs new works. *No* — the RIAA labels, GEMA, and the US Copyright Office's Part 3, which rejects the blanket transformativeness claim and reads market harm broadly. The strongest anti-AI evidence is *ROSS* (training for a competing product isn't transformative) and Munich's finding that the originals are recoverable from Suno's outputs. **The honest answer on stage: no US court has decided this for music yet.**

**2. Does a model "contain" copies of its training data?**
*Yes* — Munich Regional Court I, twice: memorisation in parameters is a reproduction, and the fact that content exists only as probability values distributed across weights is irrelevant if it can be elicited in recognisable form. *No* — the English High Court in *Getty v. Stability*: the weights "at no point contained copies," and the court endorsed the view that diffusion models store no reproductions. Two courts, two months apart, opposite technical conclusions. Technologists largely side with London; rightsholders with Munich.

**3. Is "market dilution" a legitimate fair-use theory?**
*Yes* — Judge Chhabria (nothing in copyright history has the capacity to generate millions of competing works from a fraction of the effort) and the Copyright Office. *No* — AI developers and several copyright academics: it sits awkwardly with *Warhol*, it lets plaintiffs recover for competition rather than substitution, and the licensing-market version of it is circular (you only have a licensing market if the use isn't fair).

**4. Are the label settlements a win for artists?**
*Yes* — Warner's Robert Kyncl frames licensed models plus opt-in for name, image, likeness and voice as "AI becomes pro-artist." *No* — the AFM is suing UMG and Warner for licensing members' recordings without the contractual "new use" payments, and critics argue the pattern — launch on unlicensed data, get sued, settle, license — makes infringement a **profitable go-to-market strategy** and hands the majors an equity stake in the thing that displaces their artists. Note who is *not* at the table: independent artists, session musicians, and songwriters whose publishers weren't party to the deals.

**5. Should AI-assisted output be copyrightable, and where is the line?**
*Current law* — D.C. Circuit and the Copyright Office: no human author, no copyright; prompts alone are insufficient. *The counter* — a producer iterating hundreds of prompts, curating, comping and arranging is doing recognisably authorial work, and the "prompts alone" rule is a technology snapshot the Office itself hedged with "given current generally available technology." The unresolved consequence for music: an AI-generated backing track may be **freely copyable by anyone**, which cuts against labels as much as it does against AI firms.

**6. Should the NO FAKES Act pass in its current form?**
*Yes* — RIAA, Sony Music, the major studios, and even Amazon, YouTube and OpenAI: artists need a federal right, and a patchwork of state laws is unworkable. *No* — digital-rights and open-internet groups: it builds a DMCA-style takedown regime for *speech about people*, invites over-removal of parody and commentary, and — crucially — **preempts state digital-replica laws**, potentially capping stronger protections like Tennessee's ELVIS Act.

**7. Is Japan's Article 30-4 an AI training haven?**
*The popular claim* — Japan legalised AI training on anything, including for commercial use. *The correction* — the exception fails where an "enjoyment" purpose co-exists (fine-tuning on one artist's catalogue to reproduce their sound is the textbook example), fails where use "unreasonably prejudices" the rightsholder, and does nothing at the output stage. The gap between the headline and the guidance is where most real disputes will sit.

**8. Do statutory damages produce justice or hostage-taking?**
*Deterrence* — without $150k-per-work exposure, paying licences would never beat infringing at scale. *Coercion* — class certification over ~500,000 works exposed Anthropic to a theoretical $70bn+, a number no defendant can rationally take to trial regardless of the merits. Settlements at that pressure produce no precedent, which is arguably why the law is still this unsettled after two years.

**9. Does the leaked Suno source code actually change the US case?**
*Yes* — rightsholders and MBW's framing: it removes the labels' hardest evidentiary problem. Provenance was previously an inference drawn from fingerprinting Suno's outputs and data; the manifest names YouTube Music, Deezer and Genius outright, corroborates the RIAA's stream-ripping allegation, and feeds a **DMCA §1201 anti-circumvention** claim that fair use does not answer. And *Bartz* shows the acquisition method can be the whole ballgame — $1.5bn worth.
*No, or not much* — Suno's position and a defensible litigator's read: Suno **already admitted** in August 2024 that it trained on copyrighted recordings, so the leak proves a conceded fact; the code is from 2023–24 and Suno says it is "no longer in use"; and **evidence obtained via a criminal breach and published by a journalist is not automatically admissible** — the labels would need to establish it lawfully in discovery. The fair-use question the case actually turns on is unchanged.
*The honest middle:* it probably matters more for the §1201 claim, for the 61,026-work expansion motion, and for settlement leverage than for the fair-use holding itself.

## Panel-ready questions

1. The Munich court just held that Suno's *US* training was not fair use under *US* law — a German judge applying 17 U.S.C. §107. Is that a legitimate response to the fact that AI training deliberately routes around territorial copyright, or is it a court reaching for jurisdiction it shouldn't have, and what happens when a US court reaches the opposite conclusion on the same six songs?

2. Warner and Universal sued Suno and Udio, then settled and took licensing deals and, reportedly, equity-adjacent upside. If the pattern is "launch on unlicensed data, get sued, settle, license," has litigation actually protected artists — or has it just priced the majors into the disruption while leaving independent artists and session players with nothing?

3. A track you generate entirely from a prompt has no copyright in the United States — anyone can take it and use it. Does that make AI music commercially worthless, or does it destroy the entire premise that copyright is what makes music valuable in the first place?

4. Judge Alsup said training is "exceedingly transformative." Judge Chhabria said the same plaintiffs just argued it wrong and that market dilution will usually win. Two judges in the same courthouse, two days apart. Should courts be deciding this at all, or is this Congress's job — and if Congress writes the rule, do we get a compulsory licence for music AI whether artists want one or not?

5. Tennessee's ELVIS Act lets you sue the *tool maker* whose primary purpose is cloning voices. The federal NO FAKES Act would preempt state digital-replica laws. Is a single federal standard worth trading away the strongest state protections artists currently have — and who benefits more from preemption, the artists or the platforms who endorsed the bill?

6. Three weeks ago a hacker leaked Suno's source code, and it lists YouTube Music, Deezer and Genius as training sources — two million clips from YouTube alone. Suno's answer is essentially "we told you we trained on the open internet." So: does knowing exactly *how* the audio was acquired change the copyright question at all, or is the industry's real objection that Suno was rude about it — and would anyone in this room feel differently if Suno had paid Deezer for the same files?
