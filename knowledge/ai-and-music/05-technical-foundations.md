# Technical foundations

**TL;DR for the assistant:** Almost every modern music AI does the same two-step trick: squash audio into a compact learned representation, then have a big transformer generate in that compact space. The split is whether that space is *discrete tokens* from a neural codec — SoundStream, EnCodec, DAC — which you generate autoregressively like text (MusicLM, MusicGen, and probably Suno and Udio), or a *continuous latent* you denoise all at once with diffusion or flow matching (Stable Audio, Riffusion's original spectrogram hack). Tokens give you great local texture and easy streaming but blow up in sequence length, which is exactly why long-form structure, stereo, and 44.1 kHz were the hard problems and why the newest systems compress far more aggressively — Stable Audio 3's autoencoder runs at about 10.76 latent frames per second versus MusicGen's 200 tokens per second. The dirty secret of the field is evaluation: the standard metric, Fréchet Audio Distance, is known to be biased by sample size, embedding choice, and a reference set (MusicCaps) that partly isn't even music. And watermarking is weaker than the press releases suggest — a 2025 Northwestern/Adobe paper showed neural codecs and denoisers drive detection of state-of-the-art post-hoc audio watermarks to near zero.

## Key facts

**Neural audio codecs and tokenization**

- **SoundStream** (Google, [arXiv 2107.03312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03312), 7 Jul 2021) was the first end-to-end neural codec to cover speech *and* music: a fully convolutional encoder/decoder plus a **residual vector quantizer (RVQ)**, trained with adversarial + reconstruction losses, streamable in real time on a single smartphone CPU core, spanning 3–18 kbps from one model via "quantizer dropout." At 3 kbps it beat Opus at 12 kbps in listening tests ([Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/soundstream-an-end-to-end-neural-audio-codec/)).
- **Why RVQ matters for generation:** a single vector-quantizer codebook large enough for high bitrates would need billions of entries. RVQ stacks N quantizers, each coding the *residual* error of the previous one. Side effect: audio becomes N parallel streams of integers — i.e. something a language model can predict. That's the bridge from compression to generation.
- **EnCodec** (Meta, [arXiv 2210.13438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13438), 24 Oct 2022) followed SoundStream's recipe and added a multi-scale STFT discriminator and a gradient-scale "loss balancer." It is the tokenizer under MusicGen, MAGNeT, and a lot of academic work.
- **DAC / Improved RVQGAN** (Descript, [arXiv 2306.06546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06546), 11 Jun 2023) compresses 44.1 kHz audio to ~8 kbps — about **90× compression** — using Snake periodic activations and low-dimensional factorized codebook lookup to fix EnCodec's codebook-collapse problem. The paper reports beating EnCodec at roughly 3× lower bitrate.
- Codec choice is not neutral: the DAC paper found EMA codebook learning (EnCodec's approach) gave worse SI-SDR and worse codebook utilization, and that quantizer-dropout rate 0.5 was the sweet spot between full-bitrate and low-bitrate quality.

**Autoregressive token LMs**

- **MusicLM** (Google, [arXiv 2301.11325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11325), 26 Jan 2023) is hierarchical: MuLan joint text-audio embeddings → w2v-BERT *semantic* tokens → SoundStream *acoustic* tokens, generating 24 kHz audio "consistent over several minutes." Three stages, three token types.
- **MusicGen** (Meta, [arXiv 2306.05284](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05284), 8 Jun 2023) simplified that to a **single-stage** transformer over 32 kHz EnCodec tokens: 4 codebooks at 50 Hz, generated with a "delay pattern" that offsets the codebooks so several can be predicted per step. No semantic tokens needed. Melody conditioning uses a chromagram — pitch-class energy over time — which is why you can hum a tune and keep the text-described instrumentation.
- MusicGen's context is only **30 seconds**; longer output comes from a sliding window that advances 10 s and keeps the previous 20 s as context ([AudioCraft docs](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audiocraft/blob/main/docs/MUSICGEN.md)). Long-form "structure" is therefore emergent-ish and fragile.
- MusicGen was trained on **20,000 hours** of licensed music — Meta Music Initiative Sound Collection, Shutterstock and Pond5 — with vocals stripped using HT-Demucs. Sizes 300M / 1.5B / 3.3B. Code MIT, **weights CC-BY-NC (non-commercial)** ([model card](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audiocraft/blob/main/model_cards/MUSICGEN_MODEL_CARD.md)). The card itself admits the model "cannot generate realistic vocals" and sometimes "collapses to silence" at song endings.
- **MAGNeT** (Meta/Kyutai/HUJI, [arXiv 2401.04577](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04577), 9 Jan 2024) is the non-autoregressive counterpart: mask *spans* of codec tokens and fill them in over a handful of parallel decoding steps. Reported **~7× faster** than the MusicGen autoregressive baseline at comparable quality, with a hybrid mode that generates the first seconds autoregressively then parallel-decodes the rest.

**Latent diffusion / flow matching**

- **Riffusion** (Dec 2022) was the field's most famous hack: fine-tune Stable Diffusion 1.5 on **spectrogram images**, then invert to audio ([riffusion-hobby repo](https://github.com/riffusion/riffusion-hobby), [model on HF](https://huggingface.co/riffusion/riffusion-model-v1)). It proved text-to-music was reachable with off-the-shelf image tooling, and also proved phase reconstruction from magnitude spectrograms sounds bad. Nobody serious does it that way now.
- **Stable Audio** ([arXiv 2402.04825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04825), 7 Feb 2024) introduced *timing conditioning* — you tell the model the total duration and the position of the excerpt — so a diffusion model can produce variable-length, properly-ending stereo tracks. v1 rendered up to 95 s of 44.1 kHz stereo in 8 s on an A100 with a 907M U-Net; the 2.0 line swapped the U-Net for a **diffusion transformer (DiT)**.
- **Stable Audio Open** ([arXiv 2407.14358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14358), Jul 2024) is the openly documented sibling: continuous autoencoder at a **21.5 Hz latent rate**, 64-dim latents from 44.1 kHz stereo, T5 text conditioning instead of CLAP, DiT trained on 1,024 latent tokens ≈ 47 s.
- **Stable Audio 3** ([arXiv 2605.17991](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17991), 18 May 2026; released 20 May 2026) shows where compression went: a "semantic-acoustic" autoencoder (SAME) with **4096× downsampling**, 256-dim latents at **~10.76 Hz**, DiT sizes 459M / 1.4B / 2.7B, up to **6 min 20 s** of 44.1 kHz stereo, generated in **1.31 s on an H200** in **8 sampling steps** after adversarial post-training, and no classifier-free guidance needed at inference.

**Real-time and streaming**

- **Lyria RealTime** (Google DeepMind, experimental since May 2025) streams **48 kHz stereo, raw 16-bit PCM** over a WebSocket, generating in ~2-second chunks and reacting to control changes within ~2 s ([Gemini API docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/models/lyria-realtime-exp), [Magenta announcement](https://magenta.withgoogle.com/lyria-realtime)). Controls are continuous knobs, not re-prompts: `guidance` 0–6, `bpm` 60–200, `density` 0–1, `brightness` 0–1, `scale`, `temperature` 0–3, `top_k`, plus `mute_bass` / `mute_drums` / `only_bass_and_drums`. It is **instrumental only**, and changing bpm or scale requires `reset_context()`.
- Contrast with the offline sibling: **Lyria 3** (`lyria-3-clip-preview` / `lyria-3-pro-preview`) does **44.1 kHz stereo** with generated lyrics and vocals, section tags like `[Verse]`/`[Chorus]`, image prompts, and 30 s (Clip) or "a couple of minutes" (Pro) ([docs, last updated 30 Jul 2026](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/music-generation)). Every output carries a SynthID audio watermark.
- Architectural point worth making on a panel: Lyria RealTime does *not* plan a song. It navigates a musical state space chunk by chunk, looking back a few seconds for groove and forward at the current control values. That's why it jams beautifully and can't write a bridge.

**Singing voice and voice conversion**

- Modern singing voice conversion is a **disentanglement pipeline**, not a filter: (1) a self-supervised content encoder (HuBERT / ContentVec) extracts speaker-invariant phonetic content, (2) an F0 extractor (RMVPE, formerly CREPE) gets pitch, (3) a VITS-style conditional VAE with normalizing flows produces acoustic features, (4) an NSF-HiFiGAN vocoder synthesises a **brand-new waveform**. The source singer's timbre is discarded and replaced, which is why the result doesn't sound like a pitch-shifted impression.
- **So-VITS-SVC** (SoftVC + VITS + neural source filter) was the 2023 workhorse; its main repo is now archived ([svc-develop-team/so-vits-svc](https://github.com/svc-develop-team/so-vits-svc)).
- **RVC** adds a FAISS **retrieval index** over the target singer's HuBERT embeddings, blended at inference with an `index_rate` (commonly ~0.3) to suppress "timbre leak" from the source ([Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-based_Voice_Conversion), [annotated walkthrough](https://gudgud96.github.io/2024/09/26/annotated-rvc/), [RVC WebUI](https://github.com/RVC-Project/Retrieval-based-Voice-Conversion-WebUI)). Because RMVPE tolerates polyphonic input and inference runs on 0.2–0.5 s buffers, RVC does **real-time** conversion on consumer GPUs.
- **Why voice cloning got easy, concretely:** a usable RVC model needs roughly ten minutes of clean solo vocal, which you can get by running Demucs on any commercial release; training is a one-click Colab or Applio job on a free-tier GPU; and the resulting `.pth` is a few hundred MB you can post anywhere. No lab, no budget, no consent step in the pipeline.
- **DiffSinger** ([arXiv 2105.02446](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02446), AAAI 2022) brought diffusion to score-driven singing synthesis with a "shallow diffusion" trick — start denoising partway along the trajectory from a cheap decoder's output — which is now standard in Diff-SVC, Diffusion-SVC and DDSP-SVC. Commercially the score-driven line lives on in Synthesizer V Studio 2 and ACE Studio, which are different products from Suno/Udio: you get a controllable vocal *track*, note by note, not a finished song.

**MIR building blocks**

- **Spleeter** (Deezer, Nov 2019) made stem separation a one-liner and is still the fast default in a lot of consumer tools ([repo](https://github.com/deezer/spleeter)). **Hybrid Transformer Demucs** ([arXiv 2211.08553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08553), 15 Nov 2022) replaced the innermost layers of Hybrid Demucs with self- and cross-attention across the waveform and spectrogram branches and reported **9.20 dB SDR** on MUSDB HQ with sparse attention plus fine-tuning. Caveat the repo states plainly: the sparse variant is **not** in the public release, so the headline number isn't reproducible from the shipped checkpoints ([facebookresearch/demucs](https://github.com/facebookresearch/demucs)).
- **BS-RoFormer** — band-split spectrogram + RoPE transformer — won the Music Separation track of the Sound Demixing Challenge 2023 and is generally treated as the current MUSDB18-HQ leader. [UNVERIFIED: I could not confirm a specific published SDR figure from a primary source within this session.]
- Separation is load-bearing infrastructure, not a side quest: it built MusicGen's instrumental training set, it is how people obtain clean vocals to clone, and it is how remix/karaoke products exist at all.
- **Transcription:** **MT3** (Google Magenta, ICLR 2022) frames multi-instrument transcription as seq2seq over MIDI-like event tokens ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/mt3-multi-task-multitrack-music-transcription/)); **Basic Pitch** (Spotify, [arXiv 2203.09893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.09893), [repo](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch)) is the lightweight instrument-agnostic CNN. The **2025 AMT Challenge** results ([arXiv 2603.27528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27528)) are a useful reality check: 14 submissions, only 8 produced gradeable MIDI, only 2 beat the MT3 baseline, and going from one to three simultaneous instruments cost the winner over 0.28 F-measure. Polyphonic transcription is *not* solved.
- **Beat tracking:** **Beat This!** ([arXiv 2407.21658](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21658), ISMIR 2024, [repo](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this)) beat the prior state of the art in F1 while *removing* the traditional dynamic-Bayesian-network post-processor, by training across 16 diverse datasets and using a shift-tolerant loss.
- **Embeddings:** **MERT** ([arXiv 2306.00107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00107), 95M/330M) is a BERT-style masked-prediction model over music audio using an acoustic teacher (RVQ-VAE) plus a CQT-based musical teacher; **CLAP** ([arXiv 2211.06687](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06687)) is the contrastive language-audio counterpart. **MARBLE** ([arXiv 2306.10548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10548), NeurIPS 2023 Datasets & Benchmarks) is the shared 14-task / 8-dataset yardstick. Neither embedding wins everywhere: contrastive models do better on tagging and instrument ID, MERT does better on frame-level and key/pitch tasks.

**Evaluation**

- **FAD** (Kilgour et al., [arXiv 1812.08466](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08466), Dec 2018; [Interspeech 2019](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2019/kilgour19_interspeech.pdf)) is FID for audio: fit a Gaussian to VGGish embeddings of generated audio and of a reference set, compute Fréchet distance. It was designed for *music enhancement*, not generation.
- Gui, Gamper, Braun & Emmanouilidou (ICASSP 2024, [arXiv 2311.01616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01616), toolkit [microsoft/fadtk](https://github.com/microsoft/fadtk)) is the standard citation for FAD being broken in practice: **sample-size bias** (fix: extrapolate to infinite sample size, "FAD-inf"), **embedding choice dominating the score** (VGGish correlates poorly with human judgement in some domains), and **reference-set contamination** — they specifically flag that much of MusicCaps is rated low quality by experts and some clips are music *tutorials*, not music. Their proposed replacement reference set, **FMA-Pop**, is 4,230 Free Music Archive songs (the 30 most popular in each of 163 genres).
- **MusicCaps** ([HF dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/MusicCaps)) is **5,521** 10-second YouTube/AudioSet clips captioned by ten professional musicians, ~4 sentences and ~11 "aspects" each, released with MusicLM in Jan 2023. Three structural problems for a 2026 benchmark: everything is 10 s so it says nothing about long-form structure; audio is YouTube-quality and drifts as videos are deleted; and it was an *evaluation* set that the field started treating as a *reference distribution*.
- **Subjective tests are also shaky.** The speech-synthesis literature — directly transferable — finds MOS scores are not comparable across papers, that reliable results need on the order of 30+ listeners and 150+ judgements, that naive iid error bars understate uncertainty, and that MOS is saturating as systems approach human quality ("Stuck in the MOS pit", [good-practice guide arXiv 2503.03250](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03250)). Open-source music papers routinely ship listener panels of 10–40 people.

**Watermarking and detection**

- **SynthID audio** (Google DeepMind) embeds an inaudible signal directly in the waveform of everything Lyria and NotebookLM audio produce; DeepMind states it "can't be altered by common modifications like adding noise, MP3 compression, or changing the speed of the track" ([SynthID page](https://deepmind.google/science/synthid/)). Detection is via Gemini or the SynthID Detector portal — i.e. **Google-side only**; there is no open detector.
- **AudioSeal** (Meta, [arXiv 2401.17264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17264), ICML 2024, [repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audioseal)) is the open counterpart: jointly trained generator/detector with a localization loss, detection **up to the sample level** and up to two orders of magnitude faster than prior detectors.
- **AudioMarkBench** (NeurIPS D&B 2024, [arXiv 2406.06979](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06979)) benchmarks 3 watermarks against 15 perturbations in no-box, black-box and white-box settings and concludes current schemes are vulnerable.
- The sharpest result: **"Deep Audio Watermarks are Shallow"** (O'Reilly, Jin, Su, Pardo — Northwestern + Adobe Research, [arXiv 2504.10782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10782), ICLR 2025 WMARK workshop). Post-hoc watermarks must sit at ~20–30 dB SNR to stay inaudible, and two transformation classes — **neural low-bitrate codecs and neural denoisers** — "suppress detection rates to near zero across all evaluated watermarking methods," with no knowledge of the scheme and minimal audible degradation.
- A 2025 **SoK** ([sokaudiowm.github.io](https://sokaudiowm.github.io/)) tests 9 schemes against 22 attack types in 109 configurations and concludes "none of the surveyed watermarking schemes is robust enough to withstand all tested distortions in practice," with pitch shifting and time stretching especially damaging.
- Important nuance for a panel: **SynthID audio is absent from all of these public benchmarks**, because there is no public detector to attack. Its robustness rests on Google's own testing. That is a governance problem as much as a technical one.

**Training-data provenance**

- **Stable Audio 3** publishes its corpus composition: **1,278,902 recordings** = **806,284** licensed from AudioSparx + **472,618** from Freesound (266,324 CC-0, 194,840 CC-BY, 11,454 CC Sampling+), with Freesound music screened by the PANNs tagger and sent to a third-party content-detection company to strip copyrighted material ([model card](https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-audio-3-medium), [attributions](https://info.stability.ai/attributions)). Small and Medium weights are downloadable under the Stability AI Community License; Large is API-only ([launch post](https://stability.ai/news-updates/meet-stable-audio-3-the-model-family-built-for-artistic-experimentation-with-open-weight-models)).
- The AudioSparx relationship dates to Stable Audio's Sept 2023 launch, which Stability pitched as the first commercial text-to-music model trained on licensed content ([MBW, Sept 2023](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/stability-ai-launches-text-to-music-generator-trained-on-licensed-content-via-a-partnership-with-music-library-audiosparx/)). AudioSparx openly markets its catalogue [for generative-AI training](https://www.audiosparx.com/sa/about/license-for-generative-AI-training.cfm).
- **Adobe Firefly** claims training on licensed Adobe Stock plus public-domain content and offers enterprise indemnification. The counter-example on the image side: Adobe has acknowledged that roughly **5% of Firefly's image training data was itself AI-generated** content submitted to Adobe Stock ([Creative Bloq summary of Bloomberg reporting, Apr 2024](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/adobe-firefly-trained-on-midjourney)). "Licensed" describes the paperwork, not the provenance.
- [UNVERIFIED] A musician reportedly sued Stability AI and AudioSparx in January 2026 alleging his catalogue was used for training despite opt-out requests. I could not retrieve the filing or a non-blocked report within this session; a PACER/CourtListener docket search would settle it.

**Compute, energy and carbon**

- **Nobody in music generation publishes energy numbers.** Meta's MusicGen model card has no compute, energy or carbon section at all ([model card](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audiocraft/blob/main/model_cards/MUSICGEN_MODEL_CARD.md)); the Stable Audio 3 paper gives inference latency but no training hardware, GPU hours or emissions ([arXiv 2605.17991](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17991)); Suno, Udio and Lyria disclose nothing. Hugging Face's **AI Energy Score**, the main public inference-energy benchmark, rates 10 tasks including image generation and speech recognition — **music and audio generation are not among them** ([AI Energy Score](https://huggingface.github.io/AIEnergyScore/)). Everything below is therefore derived from GPU specs and published latencies, not measured. Say so out loud if challenged.
- **The one real training figure in the open music literature** is Stable Audio Open, which does state hardware: the autoencoder trained **183 h on 32×A100**, then the decoder a further **273 h on 32×A100**, and the DiT **338 h on 64×A100** ([arXiv 2407.14358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14358)) — **≈36,200 A100-hours**. [UNVERIFIED, derived] At the A100 SXM's 400 W TDP ([NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/a100/)) and a datacentre PUE of ~1.2 that is **≈17 MWh**, about **1.6 US households' annual electricity** (10,791 kWh/yr, [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=97&t=3)); full-node accounting (CPU, network, storage) would roughly double it. And that is a *small open* model. Suno- and Lyria-scale training is presumably orders of magnitude larger, and is entirely undisclosed.
- **Per track, inference is genuinely cheap — this is the number to have ready.** [UNVERIFIED, derived] Stable Audio 3 Medium renders **6 min 20 s of 44.1 kHz stereo in 1.31 s on an H200** ([arXiv 2605.17991](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17991)). At the H200 SXM's 700 W ceiling ([NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h200/)) that is **~0.25 Wh of GPU energy, ~0.3 Wh at PUE 1.2**. Calibrate against the one *measured* figure anybody publishes: Google reports the median Gemini Apps text prompt at **0.24 Wh, 0.03 gCO₂e and 0.26 mL of water** — "less energy than watching nine seconds of television" ([arXiv 2508.15734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734), Aug 2025). **One AI-generated song is roughly one chatbot question.**
- **Now run the 90,000-tracks-a-day number.** [UNVERIFIED, derived] At 0.4 Wh a track, Deezer's ~90,000 fully AI-generated daily uploads cost **~36 kWh a day, ~13 MWh a year** — one American house. Bracket it upwards using the worst *measured* generative model of any modality: Luccioni, Jernite & Strubell clocked the least efficient image generator at **11.49 kWh per 1,000 inferences**, about half a smartphone charge per image ([arXiv 2311.16863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16863), FAccT 2024). Even at that rate, 90,000/day is **~380 MWh/yr**. Both ends are **under 0.0001%** of the **415 TWh** data centres consumed in 2024 — ~1.5% of world electricity, projected to more than double to **~945 TWh by 2030** ([IEA *Energy and AI*, 10 Apr 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai)).
- **Generation is expensive relative to other ML, but distribution dwarfs it in music.** Luccioni et al. measured **0.002 kWh** per 1,000 text classifications, **0.047 kWh** per 1,000 text generations and **2.907 kWh** per 1,000 image generations — a **>1,450× spread across 88 models** — so "generative is costly" is true in relative terms. But music already had an electricity problem: Devine (Oslo) and Brennan (Glasgow) estimated US recorded-music greenhouse emissions at **140 million kg CO₂e in 1977** (vinyl peak) and **157 million kg in 2000** (CD peak), rising to **200–350+ million kg in 2016** under streaming ([University of Glasgow, 8 Apr 2019](https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/headline_643297_en.html)). Music's footprint moved from plastic to electricity two decades before generative AI existed. [UNVERIFIED: I found no primary per-stream energy figure, so "streaming a track a few thousand times costs more than generating it" remains plausible but unquantified.]
- **Four things the cheap-per-track framing misses,** and an honest answer should concede them: (i) nobody keeps one take — Suno-style products return multiple variants per prompt and users regenerate, so energy *per delivered track* is an unknown multiple of energy per generation; (ii) 1.31 s is a vendor's own benchmark on the fastest published model of 2026, while MusicGen-era autoregressive decoding at 200 tokens/s is one to two orders of magnitude slower; (iii) efficiency is improving fast — Google reports **33× less energy and 44× less carbon** per prompt in a single year ([arXiv 2508.15734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734)) — *while absolute consumption rises*, the textbook rebound effect, and cheap generation is precisely what produces 90,000 tracks a day; (iv) the binding constraint is capacity, not marginal watt-hours. Data centres are sited, powered and cooled in units of gigawatts, and that decision is made by the training and serving fleet, not by any one song.

### The one piece of arithmetic to have ready

Raw 44.1 kHz stereo is **88,200 numbers per second**. A 6-minute song is ~32 million samples — hopeless for a transformer. MusicGen's EnCodec tokens cut that to **200 per second** mono (400 stereo), so 6 minutes ≈ **72,000–144,000 tokens**, which is why token LMs historically capped out around 30 seconds of real context. Stable Audio 3's autoencoder cuts it to **~10.76 latent frames per second**, so the same 6 minutes is **~4,100 latents** — a short sequence a diffusion transformer can attend over *all at once*. That single ratio, roughly 20–40×, is most of the answer to "why did long-form suddenly work?"

## Contested / two-sided

**1. Tokens vs. latents: which paradigm wins?**
- *Autoregressive token LMs are the future* — they inherit every LLM engineering advance (KV caching, speculative decoding, scaling laws), they stream naturally, and they handle lyrics/text as just more tokens. Suno-class products with sung lyrics are widely believed to be primarily autoregressive. Argued by the MusicLM/MusicGen lineage.
- *Diffusion/flow in a heavily compressed latent wins* — 8 sampling steps to make 6m20s ([Stable Audio 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17991)) is not something an AR model over 100k tokens can match, and the whole-sequence view gives global structure for free. Counter-counter: diffusion is awkward for open-ended streaming and for tight text/lyric alignment, and ACE-Step's authors note LM-based models still "excel at lyric alignment" while diffusion models "lack long-range structural coherence" ([arXiv 2506.00045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00045)). The 2026 reality is hybrids: an LM plans, a diffusion model renders.

**2. Is FAD good enough to compare models?**
- *Yes, with care* — it is cheap, reference-free, correlates with perception when you match the embedding to the domain, and having any shared number beats having none.
- *No* — Gui et al. show it is biased by sample size, that the embedding choice can swing perceptual correlation from ~0.1 to >0.5, and that the dominant reference set (MusicCaps) contains non-music and expert-rated low-quality audio ([arXiv 2311.01616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01616)). If a metric's ranking flips when you change the encoder, it is not measuring the generator. Practical consequence: essentially every "we beat X on FAD" claim in music generation is unfalsifiable across papers.

**3. Does watermarking actually solve provenance?**
- *Yes* — SynthID is deployed at scale on every Lyria output, survives MP3 and speed changes, and raises the cost of laundering AI audio; AudioSeal localises to the sample so you can flag a 2-second splice ([ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17264)).
- *No* — [O'Reilly et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10782) drove detection of every tested post-hoc watermark to near zero using an off-the-shelf neural codec, and the [SoK](https://sokaudiowm.github.io/) found none of 9 schemes survives all 22 attack families. Also: watermarks only mark *known* generators. Nothing marks a locally-run open-weight model, and open weights are now good (ACE-Step 1.5, Stable Audio 3 Medium). The honest framing is "watermarking is a hygiene layer for compliant platforms, not a forensic guarantee."

**4. Does "trained on licensed data" mean what buyers think?**
- *It's a meaningful distinction* — Stability publishes 1,278,902 attributions and offers indemnification; Adobe restricts to Stock + public domain. That is a real, auditable difference from scraping YouTube.
- *It's a paperwork claim, not a provenance claim* — a library-wide licence doesn't prove every individual contributor consented (the reported AudioSparx opt-out dispute [UNVERIFIED]), and Adobe's own admission that ~5% of Firefly image training data was AI-generated shows "licensed" and "clean" are different properties. Also unresolved: whether a licence to *distribute* a track implies a licence to *train* on it — most stock-library contributor agreements predate the question.

**5. Is voice cloning a technical problem or purely a policy problem?**
- *Technical mitigations exist* — voice-cloning-resistant watermarks (VoiceMark, Interspeech 2025), platform-side voice verification (Suno's Voices feature reportedly requires verification), model-side refusal of artist names in prompts (Lyria blocks prompts naming specific artists' voices).
- *No, it's over technically* — the RVC stack is open, runs on a gaming GPU, needs ten minutes of audio you can extract from Spotify with Demucs, and the archived-repo problem means there is no maintainer to pressure. Every defence assumes a compliant platform in the loop. Whatever is left is right-of-publicity law, not engineering.

**6. Is the environmental cost of AI music a real objection or a deflection?**
- *A deflection, on the arithmetic* — 1.31 s on a 700 W H200 for a six-minute track is **~0.3 Wh**, the same order as a chatbot prompt Google *measured* at 0.24 Wh ([arXiv 2508.15734](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734)). Even 90,000 tracks a day totals **13–380 MWh a year** depending on which model you assume — under 0.0001% of the 415 TWh data centres already draw ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai)). Music's actual footprint is distribution: streaming pushed US recorded-music emissions to an estimated 200–350 million kg CO₂e by 2016, above both the vinyl and CD peaks ([Glasgow, 2019](https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/headline_643297_en.html)). On carbon grounds you should object to your own listening habits long before you object to the generator.
- *A real objection, and the arithmetic is the tell* — Imogen Heap named "environmental impacts" alongside unethical training in her own framing of AI music, and the reason the counter-argument has to be *derived from GPU datasheets* is that **no music-model developer publishes a single energy figure**: not MusicGen's model card, not Stable Audio 3's paper, not Suno, and music generation isn't even a task in Hugging Face's AI Energy Score. "It's tiny" is an unaudited claim about the small half of the problem — inference — while the large half, training and the capacity buildout that serves it, is exactly what stays secret. Per-unit-is-small is also the argument that failed for every rebound-effect technology: the reason 90,000 tracks a day exist is that each one is cheap, and data centres are commissioned in gigawatts, not watt-hours per song.

## Panel-ready questions

1. **If Fréchet Audio Distance can be flipped by swapping the embedding model, and the standard reference set contains music tutorials rather than music — on what evidence should anyone believe *any* "our model is better" claim in this field? Would you accept a paper with no objective metric and 200 blind listeners instead?**
2. **Stable Audio 3 ships an attribution list of 1,278,902 recordings; Suno and Udio publish nothing. Does forced disclosure of training-set composition make models better, or does it just hand a target list to plaintiffs' lawyers and push everyone back to secrecy?**
3. **Watermarking only marks output from cooperating platforms, and a 2025 paper removed every tested open watermark with a neural codec. Is SynthID a genuine provenance layer, or is it security theatre that mostly protects Google's liability — and would you trust it more if the detector were public and attackable?**
4. **Long-form coherence got solved by compressing audio 4096× and denoising the whole song at once, rather than by teaching a model musical form. Does that mean the models now "understand" structure, or have we just built a very good texture generator with a long enough window that structure emerges statistically? Does the distinction matter to a listener?**
5. **On measured hardware, generating a six-minute track costs about as much electricity as asking a chatbot one question — roughly 0.3 watt-hours — so 90,000 AI uploads a day is a rounding error next to streaming the same catalogue to listeners. Does that retire the environmental objection to AI music, or does it collapse the moment you notice that not one music-model developer has published an energy figure and the training cost is entirely secret?**
6. **Ten minutes of a singer's isolated vocals — obtainable from any streaming release via Demucs — is enough to build a convincing voice model on a consumer GPU. Given that, is *any* technical fix worth pursuing, or should the entire response be legal? And if it's legal, what's the remedy when the model file is anonymous and hosted offshore?**

## Open questions

- **Suno and Udio architecture.** Neither has published a paper or model card. Every "inside Suno v5" write-up I found is reverse-engineered speculation, and the specs floating around contradict each other (44.1 vs 48 kHz; one site claims "175B parameters"). Nothing about their internals should be stated as fact. *What would settle it:* a technical report, or discovery documents from the label litigation.
- **BS-RoFormer's exact SDR.** Widely described as the MUSDB18-HQ state of the art and the SDX23 winner, but I could not confirm a specific dB figure from a primary source in this session. *Settled by:* the SDX23 challenge report or the BS-RoFormer paper itself.
- **SynthID audio's real robustness.** No independent benchmark exists because there is no public detector. Google's claims (survives noise, MP3, speed change) are plausible and untested by adversaries. *Settled by:* an open detection API or a published third-party red-team.
- **Riffusion's current model and corporate status.** riffusion.com now redirects through producer.ai to flowmusic.app, and the "FUZZ" model family has no accessible technical documentation. Reports of a Google Labs acquisition are secondhand. [UNVERIFIED] *Settled by:* a company statement or the model card.
- **The Stability/AudioSparx opt-out lawsuit.** Reported January 2026; I could not reach the source or the docket. [UNVERIFIED] *Settled by:* CourtListener.
- **Whether stock-library licences cover training.** No court has ruled on whether a contributor agreement written for sync/stock distribution grants training rights. This is the load-bearing assumption under both Stability's and Adobe's "clean data" positioning.
- **Per-inference energy for any music model.** Not one published measurement exists. My ~0.25–0.3 Wh per track is latency × GPU TDP × PUE, which ignores idle time, batching, the host CPU, storage and embodied carbon, and it rests on a vendor's own latency benchmark. *Settled by:* an AI Energy Score task for music generation, or one model card reporting Wh and gCO₂e per generated minute.
- **Training compute and carbon for the commercial models.** Suno, Udio, Lyria and even Stable Audio 3 disclose nothing; the only open number in the field is Stable Audio Open's ~36,200 A100-hours. *Settled by:* model cards on the Google/Meta pattern, an EU AI Act GPAI training-compute disclosure, or discovery in the label litigation.
- **Generations per delivered track.** If users take four passes and keep one, every per-track energy figure quadruples. Nobody publishes a generations-per-download ratio. *Settled by:* platform transparency reporting, or a Deezer-side study pairing upload counts with generation logs.
- **Whether any music-generation benchmark is trustworthy in 2026.** MusicCaps is 10-second YouTube clips; FAD is embedding-dependent; subjective panels run 10–40 raters. SongEval is emerging as a shared subjective framework but I did not verify its adoption breadth. *Settled by:* a MusicEval-style shared task with pre-registered protocols and 100+ raters.
